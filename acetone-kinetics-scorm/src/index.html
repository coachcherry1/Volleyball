Kinetics of the Iodination of Acetone

Method of initial rates — vary [acetone], [H+] and [I2] to find the experimental rate law

Briefing — 1 of 6

The reaction

In acid solution, iodine reacts with acetone to give iodoacetone:

CH3COCH3  +  I2  —H+→  CH3COCH2I  +  HI

H+ is a catalyst — it is not consumed. Whether it still shows up in the rate law is one of the things you are going to find out.

Briefing — 2 of 6

Why this reaction is so convenient to time

I2 is amber; everything else in the flask is colourless. So you can follow the reaction with nothing but your eyes and a stopwatch: start the clock when the iodine goes in, stop it the instant the last of the colour disappears.

A colorimeter sits on the bench alongside you, logging the absorbance at 460 nm as each run proceeds. Absorbance is proportional to concentration, so that trace is a direct picture of the iodine disappearing — and it should hit zero at the same moment your eyes say the colour has gone. You will not need to calculate anything from it; just watch its shape. You will be asked about it later.

Briefing — 3 of 6

The method of initial rates

The rate law has the form

rate = k [CH3COCH3]m [H+]p [I2]n

You cannot reason out m, p and n from the balanced equation — orders are experimental. So you run the reaction several times, changing exactly one concentration at a time and holding the other two fixed. If a concentration doubles and the rate doubles, that order is 1. If the rate quadruples, it is 2. If the rate does not budge, it is 0.

What you will do

  1. Run 1 — a baseline mixture, volumes given to you.
  2. Runs 2, 3 and 4 — you design each one to change a single reagent. The bench will not let you run a mixture that changes two things at once.
  3. Analysis — read each order off the data, check the iodine order a second way against the three integrated rate law plots, write the rate law, and calculate k.
  4. Mechanism — three mechanisms are proposed for the reaction. Only one of them predicts the rate law you measured. Work out which.
Briefing — 5 of 6

Reagents on the bench

  • 4.0 M acetone  •  1.0 M HCl  •  0.0050 M I2  •  distilled water
  • Every run is made up to exactly 20.00 mL total — water makes up the difference. That fixed total is what lets you compare runs.
  • To get a concentration in the flask, work in two steps. First find the moles of that reagent you actually delivered — volume in litres times the molarity on the bottle, so the mL and the mol/L cancel down to moles. Then divide those moles by the total volume, 0.02000 L, because that is the volume they are now spread through. Adding water changes the volume, never the moles.

Mechanism — why the rate law came out the way it did

What you measured

A balanced equation cannot tell you that. A mechanism can — it is the actual sequence of steps the reaction goes through, and the slow step sets the overall rate. Below are three mechanisms a chemist might propose for this reaction. All three add up to the same overall equation. Only one of them predicts the rate law you actually measured.

Three proposed mechanisms

Mechanism A
fast acetone + H+ → protonated acetone
slow protonated acetone → enol + H+
fast enol + I2 → iodoacetone + HI
Mechanism B
slow acetone + I2 → iodoacetone + HI
(a single step — that is the whole mechanism)
Mechanism C
fast acetone + H+ → protonated acetone
slow protonated acetone + I2 → iodoacetone + HI + H+

Reminder: the rate law comes from the slow step. If that step contains an intermediate — something made earlier in the mechanism rather than poured into the flask — it cannot stay in the rate law.
