## Check questions

One check sits in the middle of Part 2 and three make up the checkpoint, each from a different tag. A missed check brings back a different question with the same tag (at most two extra per part).

### What the kth ionization energy removes (`eq`)

**eq1.** Which equation represents the 3rd ionization energy of aluminum?

- Answer: **Al²⁺(g) + energy → Al³⁺(g) + e⁻**
- Other options: Al(g) + energy → Al³⁺(g) + 3e⁻; Al⁺(g) + energy → Al²⁺(g) + e⁻; Al³⁺(g) + energy → Al⁴⁺(g) + e⁻
- Explanation shown: Each ionization energy removes one electron. The 3rd one starts from Al²⁺, which has already lost two, and leaves Al³⁺.

**eq2.** Which equation represents the 2nd ionization energy of magnesium?

- Answer: **Mg⁺(g) + energy → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻**
- Other options: Mg(g) + energy → Mg²⁺(g) + 2e⁻; Mg(g) + energy → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻; Mg²⁺(g) + energy → Mg³⁺(g) + e⁻
- Explanation shown: The 2nd ionization energy starts from the 1+ ion and removes one more electron. Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) is the 1st.

**eq3.** A classmate says the 2nd ionization energy of sodium is the energy to remove an electron from a second sodium atom. What is wrong with that?

- Answer: **It removes a second electron from the same particle, Na⁺, not from another atom**
- Other options: Nothing. That is correct; It removes two electrons at once from one sodium atom; It puts an electron back onto Na⁺
- Explanation shown: Na⁺(g) + energy → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. The 2nd ionization energy always starts from the ion the 1st one left behind.

**eq4.** Which particle does the 4th ionization energy of silicon start from?

- Answer: **Si³⁺**
- Other options: Si; Si⁴⁺; Si⁴⁻
- Explanation shown: Three electrons are already gone, so the 4th ionization energy pulls an electron off Si³⁺ and leaves Si⁴⁺.

### Why each one costs a little more (`small`)

**sm1.** Magnesium’s first two electrons both come from occupied level 3. Why does the 2nd one (1451 kJ/mol) cost more than the 1st (738 kJ/mol)?

- Answer: **Mg⁺ is more positive. The same 12 protons now hold only 11 electrons, so each one left is pulled harder**
- Other options: Mg⁺ has more protons than Mg; Mg⁺ has fewer occupied energy levels than Mg; The 2nd electron wants to stay with the atom
- Explanation shown: Step 1 is a tie (both electrons come from level 3) and Step 2 is a tie (12 protons both times). What changed is the particle: each removal leaves it more positive, so the next electron costs a little more. A small step, not a jump.

**sm2.** Sodium’s 2nd, 3rd and 4th ionization energies (4562, 6910, 9544) all pull electrons from occupied level 2. Why does each one still cost more than the one before?

- Answer: **Each removal leaves the particle more positive, so the electrons that are left are held more tightly**
- Other options: Each removal adds a proton to the nucleus; Each electron comes from a level farther out than the last; Each removal adds a new occupied energy level
- Explanation shown: Same occupied level and the same 11 protons every time. The particle goes Na⁺, Na²⁺, Na³⁺, more positive each time, so each step goes up a little.

**sm3.** Which statement is true for every element?

- Answer: **Each ionization energy is larger than the one before it**
- Other options: The huge jump always comes after the 2nd ionization energy; Ionization energies go down once the outer level is empty; The 1st ionization energy is always the largest
- Explanation shown: Every removal leaves a more positive particle, so the cost always goes up. Where the huge jump lands depends on how many electrons are in the outer level.

**sm4.** Removing an electron from Mg to make Mg⁺ changes which of these?

- Answer: **The number of electrons, so the particle becomes more positive**
- Other options: The number of protons in the nucleus; The element: Mg⁺ is sodium; Nothing at all
- Explanation shown: The nucleus never changes during ionization. Mg, Mg⁺ and Mg²⁺ all have 12 protons. Only the electron count drops, which is why each particle is more positive than the last.

### Occupied energy levels after electrons come off (`occ`)

**oc1.** Magnesium is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s². After it loses two electrons, what has happened to energy level 3?

- Answer: **It still exists, but it is empty. Mg²⁺ has 2 occupied energy levels**
- Other options: It is gone. Mg²⁺ only has 2 energy levels; It still holds electrons. Mg²⁺ has 3 occupied energy levels; It moved closer to the nucleus
- Explanation shown: The level did not go anywhere. It just has nothing in it any more, so Mg²⁺ (1s² 2s² 2p⁶) has two occupied energy levels. That is why its next electron is so much closer in.

**oc2.** Potassium is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹. How many occupied energy levels does K⁺ have?

- Answer: **3**
- Other options: 4; 2; 1
- Explanation shown: Removing the 4s electron empties level 4, so K⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶, with 3 occupied energy levels. Level 4 still exists; it is simply unoccupied.

**oc3.** A classmate says Na⁺ has 3 energy levels because sodium is in row 3. What should they have said?

- Answer: **Na⁺ has 2 occupied energy levels. Level 3 exists, but it is empty**
- Other options: Na⁺ has 3 occupied energy levels; Na⁺ has 4 occupied energy levels because it is positive; Na⁺ has 1 occupied energy level
- Explanation shown: Na⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Energy levels exist whether or not they hold electrons. Only the occupied ones set the distance.

**oc4.** Aluminum is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹. Which of its electrons is the first one to come from occupied level 2?

- Answer: **The 4th**
- Other options: The 1st; The 2nd; The 3rd
- Explanation shown: Aluminum has 3 electrons in level 3 (3s² 3p¹). The 1st, 2nd and 3rd come from level 3. Only the 4th has to come from level 2, which is where the jump is.

### Explaining the jump with occupied levels, not size or wanting (`cause`)

**ca1.** A classmate writes: “The 3rd ionization energy of magnesium is huge because Mg²⁺ is a small ion.” What is wrong with that?

- Answer: **It uses the size as the cause. The cause is that the 3rd electron comes from occupied level 2, closer to the nucleus**
- Other options: Nothing. Small ions always hold electrons tightly; Mg²⁺ is actually a large ion; It should say Mg²⁺ has more protons than Mg
- Explanation shown: Distance means the number of occupied energy levels, never the measured radius. The small size and the huge ionization energy are both results of the same thing: the next electron is in level 2.

**ca2.** A classmate writes: “Sodium’s 2nd ionization energy is huge because Na⁺ has a full outer shell and wants to keep it.” How should it be explained instead?

- Answer: **The 2nd electron comes from occupied level 2, which is closer to the nucleus, so it is held much more tightly**
- Other options: Na⁺ has more protons than Na; Na⁺ is larger than Na; Na⁺ has gained an energy level
- Explanation shown: Atoms do not want anything. Explain it with attraction: the 2nd electron comes from the level underneath, which is closer in, and the attraction is inversely proportional to the distance squared.

**ca3.** Why is the huge jump so much bigger than the steps before it?

- Answer: **The distance changes. The electron now comes from a lower occupied level, and the attraction is inversely proportional to the distance squared**
- Other options: The nucleus gains protons at that point; The electron being removed is heavier; The particle suddenly becomes negative
- Explanation shown: Before the jump, Step 1 is a tie and only the more positive particle makes each step a little larger. At the jump the occupied level changes, so Step 1 decides, and a change in distance moves the attraction sharply.

**ca4.** Which step of the two-step rule explains the huge jump in magnesium’s ionization energies?

- Answer: **Step 1: the 3rd electron comes from a different occupied level than the 2nd**
- Other options: Step 2: Mg²⁺ has more protons than Mg⁺; Neither. The jump happens because Mg²⁺ wants a full shell; Step 2: the protons are closer together
- Explanation shown: Mg⁺ loses its electron from level 3; Mg²⁺ has to lose one from level 2. The occupied levels differ, so Step 1 settles it. The protons are 12 every time, so Step 2 never changes.

### Reading the ion and the group from the data (`ion`)

**io1.** Why does magnesium form Mg²⁺ and never Mg³⁺?

- Answer: **The 3rd electron sits past the jump. It comes from occupied level 2 and costs about 5 times as much as the 2nd**
- Other options: Magnesium wants a full outer shell; Magnesium only has 2 electrons; Mg³⁺ would have more protons than Mg²⁺
- Explanation shown: The two level 3 electrons cost 738 and 1451 kJ/mol. The 3rd has to come from level 2 and costs 7733. That jump is why magnesium stops at 2+.

**io2.** An element’s first four ionization energies are 590, 1145, 4912 and 6491 kJ/mol. Which ion does it form?

- Answer: **X²⁺**
- Other options: X⁺; X³⁺; X⁴⁺
- Explanation shown: The jump is between the 2nd and 3rd (4912 is about 4 times 1145), so there are 2 electrons in the outer level. It loses those 2 and stops: X²⁺.

**io3.** An element’s huge jump comes between its 3rd and 4th ionization energies. Which group is it in?

- Answer: **Group 13**
- Other options: Group 3; Group 4; Group 14
- Explanation shown: 3 electrons in the outer level (s² p¹) puts it in group 13. Group 3 is in the middle of the table, among the transition metals.

**io4.** An element in row 2 has these ionization energies: 519, 7298, 11815 kJ/mol. Which element is it?

- Answer: **Lithium**
- Other options: Beryllium; Sodium; Boron
- Explanation shown: The jump is right after the 1st (7298 is about 14 times 519), so there is 1 electron in the outer level: group 1. Group 1, row 2 is lithium, 1s² 2s¹.
